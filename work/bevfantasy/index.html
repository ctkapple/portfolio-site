Case Study

Bev Fantasy

A historical record book and engagement engine for three fantasy football leagues I run. It holds every season in league history and gives sixteen old friends, former teammates, and rivals across eight states a reason to keep the trash talk flowing all year.

Role
Design, product, build
Status
Live bevfantasy.com
Timeline
3 years, on and off

A league is a group chat with a scoreboard attached

Fantasy football is the thing that kept sixteen friends talking after everyone began to scatter across the country, and it is now, besides weddings, the only reliable way to get everyone in the same room.

The problem is that a fantasy platform is built around the season you are currently in. It is not built to be a filing cabinet or to keep a group chat active year-round. Once a season ends, group chat activity dies down as we return to the mundanity of adult life. Records, rivalries, the trade everybody still argues about, all of it survives in the memory of whoever tells the story loudest and least accurately.

So the league had a memory problem and an engagement problem, and they turned out to be one problem. Group chat traffic in July is the honest measure of whether a league is healthy, and ours went quiet every February.

What I optimized for

The API will hand you everything you could ever need and more. The failure mode for a project like this is a wall of accurate tables that technically answers every question and gets opened twice.

I optimized for things worth screenshotting. Every feature had to survive one test before I built it: Would somebody paste this into the group chat to make fun of a friend? That sounds unserious, and it was the most useful constraint I had. It killed a lot of correct, boring ideas, and it is why the things that shipped actually get used.

Feedback from the league shaped nearly every feature: what people wanted to see, how they expected it to work, and what was not worth keeping. That process turned the site into a frequently used league repository, visited not only by league members but by their friends and families.

Act one

The record

What the league did, kept somewhere it can be found and argued with.

Money, in the language they already speak

Most of this league works in finance. So instead of another standings table, everyone got a ticker. Mine is $WILL, and you'll notice it's at the top. The page is an exchange: a scrolling tape of lifetime winnings along the top, a cumulative equity curve underneath, and a holdings table breaking every dollar down by year and by league.

The tape is what made it land. A total is a fact and a number that moves is a story, so each symbol carries the season's change beside it, green if the year cleared your buy-in and red if it did not.

The ledger runs back to 2021 and nets every buy-in ever paid, which is the number most of them would rather not look at.

A hundred trades and no way to find one

Every time a trade goes down, the whole league calls it a fleece, regardless of what it contains. Down the road, I want to add a Fleece Score so every trade carries a rating for just how lopsided it was.

Trades are the best content a league produces and the hardest thing to pull up in an instant. Every trade argument in our group chat starts the same way, with somebody reconstructing a deal from memory and getting the details horribly wrong.

A reverse chronological list of a hundred trades is an archive, not a tool, so the whole design problem was the filter bar. It stacks free-text search over the four dimensions people actually think in: season, position, who was involved, and what changed hands.

The manager control took the most thought. The obvious version filters to trades involving one person. The version people immediately wanted allows a second name, because the real question isn't "show me my trades"; it's "how many times have I fleeced Adam?" Draft picks and FAAB are their own filters rather than search terms, since "every trade where somebody moved a first" is a different question from a player's name, and it is the one that starts fights.

Ammunition

Head to head gets screenshotted more than anything else on the site. It was also the first and least sophisticated thing I built. Unsurprisingly, I built it to settle an argument.

You pick two faces from a grid. No dropdowns, no names to type. That choice does more work than it looks like it does: selecting someone by their face is fast enough that people browse, and browsing is how anyone finds out that a rivalry they never thought about is 4-2.

What people came for is the biggest blowout with its margin and when it happened, the closest game the two of them have ever played, and a line I called Certified Hater: the player on the other roster who has personally scored the most points against you. I wrote it purely to prove that Ja'Marr Chase has a grudge against me personally.

None of it helps anyone win a game. All of it gets pasted into the group chat, which was the entire point.

Act two

The engine

Two features built at specific people who had stopped participating, for specific reasons I could name.

Manufacturing an event

College football has a ritual. The AP Poll drops, and for the next 24 hours everyone argues about it. Fantasy has no equivalent. That is especially true in dynasty leagues, where roster construction can make some managers' seasons feel decided before Week 1. We used to rely on automated power rankings. They had their merits, but the real breakthrough was letting the league vote.

So I built one. Every manager ranks all fourteen teams, ballots close, and the poll publishes with movement arrows against the previous one. Ranking fourteen teams is genuinely tedious, though, and a ritual nobody finishes is not a ritual. Three things fixed completion.

  • The ballot uses drag-to-rank and works smoothly with a thumb, because it usually gets filled out on a couch or on the toilet.
  • Selecting a team opens its core players beside the ballot, so ranking honestly does not mean leaving the site to check a roster and losing your place.
  • An open poll announces itself across every page, showing how many ballots are in and linking directly to the ballot.

None of that was right the first time. Before the poll went to the league I put the build in front of a handful of the die-hards, the people who would use it every week, and let them pull it apart.

Too much on screen at once, came back one reply, and why not publish a top seven the way college football does. Someone else caught that the ballot counter read 1 of 14 when the league has fourteen teams but sixteen people, because two franchises are co-owned. Both went in. The published poll is a top seven now, sitting under the full chart rather than replacing it, and the counter reads out of sixteen.

The bar is dismissible and remembers that you dismissed it. Showing the count rather than the deadline is what moved the needle, because nobody wants to be the last one in.

The two people who never played

Every league has members who show up less. Our keeper league had two who had gone four straight years without submitting their keepers before the draft.

The easy read would be that they did not care, but the real reason was simpler and more fixable. The keeper rules are eight accumulated bylaws covering cost basis, consecutive year limits, undrafted players priced off end-of-season ADP, and what happens when you do not hold the pick you need. Working out whether you were even allowed to keep someone took twenty minutes, and there was still a decent chance of being wrong.

The Keeper Assistant reads your roster and answers the question. Pick your team and it shows every player you own with their cost basis, the round they would cost next year, how many consecutive years they have already been kept, and a plain yes or no for keeper eligibility.

Both managers set full keepers the first year it existed. It is the only feature I have shipped where I could name the two people it was for before I wrote a line of it.

How it evolved

Before Bev Fantasy, we had other fantasy leagues but no dedicated site. I started the keeper league in 2021 and built the admittedly terrible Wix site that same year. In 2023, I built the first version of Bev Fantasy and have been iterating on it ever since.

The site now refreshes from Sleeper's API every night, so its history and transaction log stay current without anyone maintaining them. Three leagues share one codebase, and adding a fourth takes one config file.

The blogs were fun to write, but the time and energy to write them evaporated as I got older. Automated stat tracking, convenient tooling, and visually interesting and useful graphs have done a much better job of keeping engagement high without giving me arthritis.

What I got right was the constraint. Optimizing for what people would send each other, rather than for coverage, is why the pages that get traffic are the poll, head to head and trades, and not the statistically richer views sitting next to them in the nav.

What I got wrong, repeatedly, is that I still ship things nobody asked for. The Keeper Assistant worked because I watched two specific people fail at a specific task for four years. The features that flopped are the ones where I found interesting data and went looking for a reason to show it. Knowing the difference in advance is the part I am still bad at.

The mark

The Bev Fantasy wordmark was designed by Peter Coluntino, a graphic designer, friend, and league mate. The brief I gave him was a mockup I had thrown together myself, which is the first screenshot below: our old high school panther, the word FANTASY in a typeface I would rather not defend, and an offer, or really a plea, to ignore all of it.

When I work with artists, I prefer to let them cook, then respond to the work in front of me with clear, actionable feedback. I can be headstrong about the original vision, but the best results usually come when I work closely with people who know their craft better than I do.

Everything else is the orange and black of the high school team most of us played on together, which won two state championships and which none of us has stopped talking about since.
