Case Study

bevfantasy

A record book and engagement engine for three fantasy football leagues run by the same group of friends. It holds every season since 2021 and gives fourteen people now spread across eight states a reason to keep showing up for each other in July. It is live, and the people using it tell me directly when something is wrong.

Role
Design, product, build
Status
Live (bevfantasy.com)
Timeline
3 years, on and off

A league is a group chat with a scoreboard attached

Fantasy football was never really about fantasy football for us. It was the thing that kept fourteen friends talking after everyone left the same town, and it is now the only standing appointment a group spread across eight states reliably keeps.

The problem is that a fantasy platform is built around the season you are currently in, and that is the right thing for it to be built around. It is not built to be a filing cabinet. Once a year closes, the year effectively disappears. Records, rivalries, the trade everybody still argues about, all of it survives only in whoever remembers it loudest.

So the league had a memory problem and an engagement problem, and they turned out to be one problem. When nobody can look anything up, nobody has anything to say. Group chat traffic in July is the honest measure of whether a league is healthy, and ours went quiet every February.

What I optimized for

Not completeness. The API will hand you far more than anyone wants to read, and the failure mode for a project like this is a wall of accurate tables that technically answers every question and gets opened twice.

I optimized for things worth screenshotting. Every feature had to survive one test before I built it: would somebody paste this into the group chat to make fun of a friend. That sounds unserious, and it was the most useful constraint I had. It killed a lot of correct, boring ideas, and it is why the things that shipped actually get used.

Act one

The record

What the league did, kept somewhere it can be found and argued with.

Money, in the language they already speak

Most of this league works in finance. So instead of another standings table, everyone got a ticker. I am $WILL, Kevin Flaherty is $FLTZ, and the page is an exchange: a scrolling tape of lifetime winnings across the top, a cumulative equity curve underneath, and a holdings table below that breaking every dollar down by year and by league.

The tape is what made it land. A total is a fact and a number that moves is a story, so each symbol carries the season's change beside it, green if the year cleared your buy-in and red if it did not.

These same people play in three different leagues, so the chart toggles between one league and all of them. It animates instead of reloading. Nothing is created or destroyed when you switch, rows just move, which matters because the only thing anyone actually reads on this chart is whether their line is above someone else's.

Five seasons, three leagues, one ledger. Net and ROI are computed against every buy-in ever paid, which is the number people would rather not look at.

Ninety trades and no way to find one

Trades are the best content a league produces and the hardest thing to retrieve. Every trade argument in our group chat starts the same way, with somebody reconstructing a deal from memory and getting it wrong.

A reverse chronological list of ninety trades is an archive, not a tool, so the whole design problem was the filter bar. It stacks free text search over the four dimensions people actually think in: season, position, who was involved, and what changed hands.

The manager control took the most thought. The obvious version filters to trades involving one person. The version people wanted takes an optional second name, because the real question is never "show me my trades," it is "how many times have these two traded and who came out ahead." Draft picks and FAAB are their own filters rather than search terms, since "every trade where somebody moved a first" is a different question from a player's name, and it is the one that starts fights.

The result count updates live above the list. Showing 7 of 90 sounds trivial and is the thing that makes the bar feel like browsing rather than querying.

Ammunition

Head to head gets screenshotted more than anything else on the site, and it is the least sophisticated thing I built.

You pick two faces from a grid. No dropdowns, no names to type. That choice does more work than it looks like it does: selecting someone by their face is fast enough that people browse, and browsing is how anyone finds out that a rivalry they never thought about is 2-6.

Under the all-time record, the stats are deliberately not the useful ones. Total and average points are there because they should be. What people came for is the biggest blowout with its exact margin and week, the closest game the two of them have ever played, and a line I called Certified Hater: the player on the other roster who has personally scored the most points against you.

None of it helps anyone win a game. All of it gets pasted into the group chat, which was the entire point.

Act two

The engine

Two features built at specific people who had stopped participating, for specific reasons I could name.

Manufacturing an event

College football has a ritual. The poll drops, and for an hour everyone argues about it. Fantasy has no equivalent. Nothing on the calendar gathers the whole league at one moment for a reason other than their own matchup.

So I built one. Every manager ranks all fourteen teams, ballots close, and the poll publishes with movement arrows against the last one. Ranking fourteen teams is genuinely tedious though, and a ritual nobody finishes is not a ritual. Three things fixed completion.

The ballot is drag to rank and works with a thumb, because it gets filled out on a couch. Selecting a team opens that team's current roster and projected core right beside the ballot, so ranking honestly no longer means leaving the site to go check somebody's roster and losing your place. And an open poll announces itself: a bar sits across every page of the site while voting is live, showing how many ballots are in.

The bar is dismissible and remembers that you dismissed it. Showing the count rather than the deadline is what moved the needle, because nobody wants to be the last one in.

The two people who never played

Every league has members who show up less. Ours had two who had gone four straight years without submitting a full keeper selection before the draft.

The easy read is that they did not care. The real reason was simpler and more fixable. The keeper rules are eight accumulated bylaws covering cost basis, consecutive year limits, undrafted players priced off end-of-season ADP, and what happens when you do not hold the pick you need. Working out whether you were even allowed to keep someone took twenty minutes and a decent chance of being wrong. So they did not bother.

The assistant reads a roster and answers the question. Pick your name and you get every player you own with their cost basis, the round they would cost next year, how many consecutive years they have already been kept, and a plain yes or no.

The part I am most pleased with is where it refuses to answer. Three of the eight rules involve trading for picks, which depends on deals that have not happened yet, so it does not model them and says so in plain language under the table. It also flags when a computed cost falls off the end of the draft, and when you do not currently hold the pick a keeper would consume. In a league that argues about everything, being confidently wrong once would have ended the tool.

Both managers set full keepers the first year it existed. It is the only feature I have shipped where I could name the two people it was for before I wrote a line of it.

Where it landed

The site is a static build that refreshes from the league platform's API every night, so the history stays current without anyone maintaining it. Three leagues share one codebase and adding a fourth is adding one config file. Before this there was a free-tier website I made six years ago when the league started, which was worse in every respect and which nobody, including me, ever opened twice.

What I got right was the constraint. Optimizing for what people would send each other, rather than for coverage, is why the pages that get traffic are the poll, head to head and trades, and not the statistically richer views sitting next to them in the nav.

What I got wrong, repeatedly, is that I still ship things nobody asked for. The Keeper Assistant worked because I watched two specific people fail at a specific task for four years. The features that flopped are the ones where I found interesting data and went looking for a reason to show it. Knowing the difference in advance is the part I am still bad at.

The BEV mark was designed by Peter Coluntino, who is also in the league. Everything else is the orange and black of the high school team most of us played on together, which won two state championships and which none of us has stopped talking about since. The screens here show real league data, published with the league's blessing.
