Case Study

Yggdrasil

An org chart explorer, designed and built for internal use. It turned a structure most people navigated by asking around into something they could open and read. It is live inside the organization today, though not publicly deployed.

The chart existed. Nobody could use it.

Reporting lines lived in one internal system, roles and locations in a second, skills and subject matter expertise in a third. The three never quite agreed, and none of them answered the question people were actually asking, which was some version of "who owns this, and who do I talk to about it."

So people asked a manager. The manager pointed them at someone else, and that person pointed them somewhere else again. New hires got the worst version of it, because they did not yet know enough to know who to ask. Every partner team, every cross department project, every onboarding week paid the same small tax.

Scale is what made it stick. The organization is one group inside a company of more than five thousand people, so reporting lines rarely stay tidy. They cross into neighbouring groups and back again, and the person who actually owns a thing is often several steps from anyone you already know. A chart that only covers your immediate surroundings does not help with that, and a chart that covers everything is unreadable.

Why a tree, and why this one

The name comes from the world tree at the center of Norse cosmology, the thing every realm hangs off. An org chart is a tree too, so the reference did real work rather than sitting on top as a skin. Drawn radially, the whole organization fits in one frame. Distance from the center reads as distance from leadership, colour reads as team, and the density of a branch tells you how large a group is before you read a single name.

The look followed from the same idea: dark field, luminous nodes, teams named for realms. I focused heavily on brand and aesthetic design because even the most useful information gets lost if people do not want to engage with it.

Two views, two jobs

Tree is for orientation. It answers "where does this sit" and "how big is that group really," and it is the view people screenshot and paste into a channel.

Directory is for lookup. Same data, familiar table, grouped by team with role, location, manager and report count on every row.

Neither one is a mode you get stuck in. Any person in the directory can be sent to their place in the tree, and any node in the tree carries the same facts the table row does.

Depth on demand

Selecting a team does two things at once. The tree collapses to a constellation of just that group, so a whole-organization picture becomes a readable one, and a panel opens with what that team is actually responsible for.

Those responsibilities were the part no system held before. They lived in team leads' heads and in onboarding docs that went stale.

What goes on a person

The individual view was the piece that replaced the three tab habit, so what went on it mattered more than how it looked. Rather than argue it out, the field set went to a pilot group as an A/B test before full rollout, and the winning arrangement is what shipped: title, team, location, manager, direct reports, and one click copy for name and email.

Direct reports earned their place. Fully expanded, a manager's node fans out into every person under them, which turned out to be how people read team size and load without opening anything else.

Keeping it true

An org chart is only as good as its last update, so the tool asks. Once a quarter it messages everyone in the organization messaging tool to confirm their details and refresh their subject matter expertise tags. The data stays current because maintaining it is a two minute reply rather than a project.

Where it landed

From idea to implementation, rollout, and adoption took less than two weeks. It later expanded into an organization-wide home page and internal site with highly specific tooling, shortcuts, and automations requested by senior management.
